# The copywriting canon, as checks

One working idea per writer, paraphrased, and the check it turns into. Use it to pick an angle or to work out why a line is weak.

## Find the true detail

- **Claude Hopkins** (*Scientific Advertising*, 1923). Specific claims get believed and general claims slide past. At Schlitz he described the steam-cleaned bottles that every brewery used, and Schlitz got credit for it because nobody had said it before. Check: does the line carry a detail that is true, concrete, and not yet said by anyone else in this market?
- **David Ogilvy** (*Confessions of an Advertising Man*, 1963). Research first. The famous Rolls-Royce headline about the electric clock being the loudest noise at 60 mph came from a motoring journalist's road test. He also said the reader isn't a moron. Check: did you dig for the most interesting true fact before you wrote, and does the line respect the reader's intelligence?
- **Harry Dry** (Marketing Examples). Three tests: can you picture it, can it be proved true or false, and could nobody else say it? Three no's means the line is filler. Check: run all three on every option.

## Start from the reader

- **Robert Collier** (*The Robert Collier Letter Book*, 1931). Join the conversation already going on in the reader's head. Check: what is the visitor wondering when they reach this line, and does the line answer that?
- **Eugene Schwartz** (*Breakthrough Advertising*, 1966). Copy can't create desire. It points desire that already exists at a product. How aware the reader is decides the lead: someone unaware of the problem needs the problem or a story, and someone comparing options needs the proof or the offer. Check: name the reader's awareness level and lead with what that level needs.
- **Gary Halbert** (*The Boron Letters*). The audience matters more than the copy. He would pick a starving crowd over better burgers. Check: is this line written for a specific reader who wants what's on offer, or for everyone?
- **Joanna Wiebe** (Copyhackers). The best phrases come from customers. Mine reviews, interviews, support tickets and sales calls for their exact words, then use them with as little paraphrase as you can. Check: can any phrase in the line be traced to something a customer or client actually said?
- **Ann Handley** (*Everybody Writes*, 2014). Write the ugly first draft, then edit from the reader's side with empathy. Check: reading as the visitor, why should they care?

## Make it work as copy

- **John Caples** (*Tested Advertising Methods*, 1932). The headline does most of the work, so write many and let tests pick. His "They laughed when I sat down at the piano" promised a story the reader wanted to finish. Check: did you write enough distinct headlines that the best one had competition?
- **Joseph Sugarman** (*The Adweek Copywriting Handbook*). The first sentence exists to get the second one read, and each line should pull the reader into the next. Check: does the first line make the reader want the second?
- **Dan Kennedy** (*The Ultimate Sales Letter*). Problem, agitate, solve: name the problem, make it felt, then present the fix. Check: for selling copy, is the problem named in the reader's terms before the solution shows up?

## Personality that carries a fact

- **Bill Bernbach and Paula Green** (DDB, Avis, 1962). "We try harder" admitted Avis was number two and turned that into the reason to pick it. Volkswagen's "Lemon" ad did the same with a rejected car. An honest admission earns trust that bragging can't. Check: can a flaw, told honestly, become the proof of the virtue?
- **Mailchimp content style guide** (Kate Kiefer Lee). "We have a sense of humor, but we value clarity over entertainment." Check: if the joke is removed, is the line still clear and true?
- **Kinneret Yifrah** (*Microcopy: The Complete Guide*). The voice stays fixed and the tone moves with the moment. Being funny doesn't automatically make microcopy better. Check: does this tone fit what the user is feeling at this moment, such as an error, a payment or an empty state?
- **Luke Sullivan** (*Hey Whipple, Squeeze This*). Simple beats clever, and an idea that isn't great should die even if you love it. Check: would a stranger get it in one read?
