# Copywriting

Good copy is a true, specific fact told so the reader cares. Most of the work is finding the fact. The words come after, and they come as several options, not one.

Three leading words run through this skill. The **fact sheet** is what you know to be true. An **angle** is one distinct way into a line. The **pick** is the one option you ship, with reasons.

## Fast process for short copy

Use this for a headline, subline, tagline, bio, CTA, quote or a single section. A page is a set of these units. Run the process per unit, then read the page once top to bottom. For one line, the whole process fits in one reply. Ask at most once, in one batch, only for facts that block the line, and send a first pick built from what you have alongside the questions.

1. **Diagnose the input.** Decide which case you're in. A blank or generic brief gives you room on the angle, never on the facts. A draft with a real voice or angle gets restored: keep its argument and its words, and fix the buried lead, vague lines and tells. A rejected draft, or one that reads flat or AI-written, is a blank brief whose facts you keep and whose words you drop. A point edit changes only the line asked about. Done when you can name the case in one line with the evidence for it.
2. **Build the fact sheet.** List what is true and specific: names, numbers, what was built or done, for whom, and what customers or clients said, word for word, with the source. Mine customer words for verbs, before-states, unprompted results and objections. Read the sibling copy the line sits beside, such as the other team cards or the hero, and note its length, person and shape. A trait the brief states counts as a fact. A habit or anecdote you infer does not. Done when every claim you might use traces to the brief, the repo or the user's words. A line that needs a fact you lack gets a question or a `[NEED: what fact, from whom]` flag. With nobody to ask, write from what you have and keep the flags.
3. **Name the reader and the moment.** One sentence: who reads this line, what they are wondering when they reach it, and what they already know. A team card reaches a prospective client deciding whether to trust these people. An error message reaches someone whose task just broke. Done when the sentence names the reader, the moment and the question the line answers.
4. **Write options across angles.** Write 6 to 10 options for a headline or tagline, and 4 to 6 for a bio, subline, CTA or section. Give each option its own angle, and count each warm-turn device as a separate angle. Two options on one angle count as one unless they use different facts. A headline, subline or CTA gets at most two warm-turn options, and none where money, data or health is at stake.
   - **Plain.** The fact, said simply.
   - **Proof.** The most specific true detail, the one nobody else in the market has said yet.
   - **Customer words.** A phrase lifted from what a customer or client actually said. With no quotes on hand, skip it and flag the quote that would help.
   - **Reader's problem.** The problem in the reader's own terms, then the answer.
   - **Warm turn.** The fact told with personality, using the rule below.
   - **Against the category.** A choice from the fact sheet that the category usually skips, stated as this business's positive.

   Done when the options cover at least four angles, or three for a bio or CTA, and one of them is a plain or proof option for the others to beat. Microcopy, including in-product buttons, and quotes work differently: write three or four options that vary what comes first, which next step is offered, or which part of the quote is kept. In a restored draft or a point edit, the options vary the fix, not the angle.
5. **Score, then pick.** Run every option through the tests below and drop the ones that fail. Pick one, give two or three reasons tied to the tests and the reader's moment, and name a runner-up with when you'd use it instead. Done when the pick passes every test. When nothing passes, the fact is the problem, so go back to step 2 for a better one rather than polishing words. [`CANON.md`](CANON.md) has one check per classic copywriter for working out why a line is weak. CTAs, microcopy and quotes skip the proof, nobody-else and billboard tests.
   - Can the reader picture it?
   - Can it be proved true or false?
   - Could nobody else say it?
   - Is every claim true against the fact sheet, including what it promises?
   - Does it answer the reader's question at that moment?
   - Does it match its siblings, within a few words of their length and in the same shape, without copying their punchline?
   - Does it stand on its own? A headline should work on a billboard. A subline, card or error should make sense with only its heading or name beside it.
   - Is it free of the tells in `unslop` and the copy tells below?
6. **Leave it alone where it works.** For a rewrite, check each change: measurably better, or only different? Revert the ones that are only different. Done when every surviving change fixes a named test failure.

Reply in this shape:

```text
Reader: <who reads it, at what moment, asking what>
Pick: <the line>
Why: <two or three reasons>
Runner-up: <the line>, for <when you'd use it>
Options: <each option, its angle, and the test it failed or "passes">
Needs: <any [NEED: ...] flags>
```

Give one block per unit. For a page, add a closing note on anything two units repeat or contradict. A point edit returns only the changed line and one reason.

## The warm turn: from fact to a playful line

This is the rule for a line that is human and playful, shows care, and stays true. It matters most for bios, team cards and about pages, where flat or clever-but-empty copy does the most damage.

1. **Start from the fact.** What the person or product actually did, from the fact sheet.
2. **Find the care behind it.** Ask what the fact proves about the person: the trait or motive a client would be glad of. Someone who rewrote a checkout cares that buyers don't get stuck. Someone with high standards cares that the experience is right. Use the care to choose the picture, and state it in the line only when the brief does.
3. **Find the picture.** Translate the mechanism into what it looks like from outside. A visitor can't picture a caching layer, but they can picture a page that opens before they've finished tapping. Name the effect, not the internals, and only an effect the facts back, such as a measured load time.
4. **Add one light turn.** Pick one device:
   - **The virtue told as a flaw.** Admit the quirk and let it be the reason to trust them. Avis said "We try harder" about being number two. A team card on the TrueDevs About page reads "Leads experience and UX. Not easy to satisfy, which is the point."
   - **Personification.** The product or system does something human.
   - **Understatement.** Say less than the fact, and let the fact do the bragging.
   - **The odd true detail.** One specific detail so precise it charms, like Ogilvy's electric clock.
   - **The teammate's tease.** What their colleagues would affectionately rib them about.
5. **Pass the truth gate: exaggerate the feeling, never the record.** The attitude can stretch for effect when the facts prove it and no reader would take the stretch literally. Anything a reader could check stays literally true: what was built, for whom, the numbers, and every promise about outcomes such as uptime, speed, "never", "always" or "before you notice". Habits, anecdotes and motives you infer need the person's confirmation before they appear.
6. **Pass the care check.** The reader should feel looked after, and the subject should be glad to share the line. A tease the subject would wince at fails.

Ration the turn. One device per line, and most lines on a page stay plain. Bios and team cards are the exception, where every card gets its warm line. Headlines and CTAs usually earn their warmth through a specific fact rather than a joke.

## Formats

- **Headlines and sublines.** The headline carries the one thing. The subline adds the specific proof the headline couldn't fit. Lead with what the reader's awareness needs: the problem for someone who doesn't know they have it, the proof or offer for someone comparing options. The first line exists to get the second one read.
- **CTAs.** A verb plus what the reader gets: "Book a 20-minute call", "Get the price list". Urgency appears only when it is real.
- **Bios and team cards.** Role first, plainly, because visitors scan for who does what. Then one human line from the warm turn. Match the siblings' length, person and rhythm, and give each card its own device so the set doesn't read as one template.
- **Quotes and testimonials.** Edit real words, and leave new ones unwritten. Compress what the person said, keep their meaning and their best phrase, attribute it, and get their approval before it ships. Fixing case, spelling and filler words is editing. A new claim is not. A customer's story reads as a promise once it's on the page, so keep the details the business offers everyone, or confirm the rest first. With no quote on hand, flag `[NEED: quote from <who> about <what>]`.
- **Microcopy.** Buttons name the result: "Save draft", "Send invoice". Errors say what happened and what to do next, framed as a constraint rather than blame: "Names use lowercase letters, numbers and hyphens." Empty states give the one next step. Destructive confirmations state the consequence. Tone follows the user's moment, so errors that cost money or data stay plain.

## Copy tells

`unslop` always applies and covers the general AI tells. In copy, its rule to name the mechanism means the effect the reader sees, not the internals. For microcopy, use its patterns and leave its advice on adding soul to prose. These are the tells specific to copy, each with its fix:

- **The wisdom-shaped line.** An aphorism that could hang on any office wall. Replace it with the fact it gestures at.
- **The tidy reversal.** A setup with a mirrored payoff, such as "Simple to start, impossible to outgrow." It sounds clever and shows nothing. Replace it with one picture.
- **Spec-sheet nouns.** Internals the reader can't picture, like caching layers, pipelines or schemas. Name the effect the reader would notice.
- **Stacked fragments.** "Fast. Simple. Yours." Write one sentence that says the specific thing.
- **Hollow superlatives and invented numbers.** "Best", "leading", "10x". Use the real number or flag `[NEED: ...]`.
